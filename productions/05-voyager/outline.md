# Video 05 outline: Voyager 1 (owner chose the topic 2026-10-29)

**Title (draft):** Voyager 1: The Farthest Thing Humans Ever Made | Hindi Documentary
**Publish target:** before or on **18 Nov 2026**, the day Voyager 1 is one light-day from Earth (NASA's date).
**Length:** about 1,600 words, about 11 min at 1.0x.
**The one question:** इंसान की बनाई सबसे दूर की चीज़ वहाँ तक कैसे पहुँची, और जब उसकी आवाज़ हमेशा के लिए बंद होगी, तब उसके साथ क्या जाएगा?
**Our angle (not Dhruv Rathee's 2024 "aliens" video):** the life and slow goodbye of a 49-year-old machine,
with the 2023-24 rescue and the one-light-day milestone.

Each chapter lists the context the viewer gets *before* the new idea (the trial rule "context before
concept"), and the real footage or voice it can use (all NASA/JPL, public domain; to be confirmed clip by clip).

## Opening (40-50 s): the day Voyager stopped making sense
- Context first: a spacecraft launched in 1977, today the farthest thing humans have ever made.
- November 2023, JPL, California. Voyager 1 suddenly starts sending the same meaningless pattern, again
  and again. A question takes 22.5 hours to reach it and the answer another 22.5. Many of the people who built
  it are retired; the manuals are on paper.
- Tease, not solved yet (it pays off in chapter 7): what happened next took five months.
- Second tease: on 18 November 2026 it becomes the first human-made object one light-day from Earth.
- The question, then the channel intro and title card.
- Footage: JPL mission control (NASA), the Deep Space Network dishes (NASA).

## Ch1 THE CHANCE OF 175 YEARS (Gary Flandro, 1965)
- Context: in the 1960s, a direct flight to Neptune would have taken about 30 years: too long for any machine.
- Idea: a young JPL engineer, Gary Flandro, finds that in the late 1970s Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune
  line up, once in about 175 years.
- Comparison: gravity assist is like a ball that gains speed when it bounces off a moving bat; each planet throws
  the spacecraft onward.
- Payoff: Neptune in 12 years instead of 30. The window would not come again until the 2150s.
- Footage: archive animation of the Grand Tour (NASA), a photo of Flandro if one is free.

## Ch2 TWO SHIPS, 1977
- Context: why two (one could fail), and why Voyager 2 left first (20 Aug) but Voyager 1 (5 Sep) took the faster
  path and overtook it.
- Idea: the power. Sunlight out there is too weak for solar panels (at Jupiter about 25 times weaker), so each
  Voyager carries plutonium that makes heat, and the heat makes electricity.
- Payoff: that plutonium is why Voyager still works today, and why it is dying (chapter 8).
- Also: computers with less memory than a single phone photo, data on an 8-track-style tape (exact figures to
  check).
- Footage: the real Titan-Centaur launches (NASA, 1977), assembly in the JPL clean room.

## Ch3 A RECORD FOR STRANGERS (the Golden Record)
- Context: these two ships would leave the solar system and never come back, so they could carry a message.
- Idea: Carl Sagan's team makes a gold-plated record: about 115 pictures, sounds of Earth, 90 minutes of music,
  greetings in 55 languages, and the instructions to play it engraved on the cover.
- The Indian moment: the Hindi greeting "धरती के वासियों की ओर से नमस्कार", and Surshri Kesarbai Kerkar's
  Bhairavi "जात कहाँ हो", the only Indian music on it.
- Payoff: almost certainly no one will ever find it, and that was never the point; it is humanity's self-portrait,
  built to last about a billion years.
- Footage: the record and its cover (NASA/JPL), Sagan with the team (NASA). Real audio: the greetings if their
  licence is clean; the music recordings are copyrighted, so they are named, never played.

## Ch4 WORLDS NO ONE HAD SEEN
- Context: before 1979 scientists expected the moons of the outer planets to be cold, dead rocks.
- Jupiter, March 1979: a navigation engineer, Linda Morabito, sees a strange cloud at the edge of Io: the first
  active volcano found anywhere beyond Earth.
- Saturn, 1980: Voyager 1 is sent close to Titan, a moon with a thick atmosphere. The price: that bend throws it
  out of the planets' plane, so it can never visit another planet.
- Voyager 2 goes on alone: Uranus 1986, Neptune 1989. Still the only visits those planets have ever had.
- Footage: the real Voyager image sequences of Jupiter, Io, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune and Triton (NASA/JPL),
  the 1989 Neptune encounter at JPL.

## Ch5 THE PALE BLUE DOT (14 Feb 1990)
- Context: Voyager 1's cameras were about to be switched off forever to save power.
- Idea: Carl Sagan asks for one last picture, looking back. From about 6 billion km, Earth is less than one
  pixel, inside a ray of sunlight.
- Payoff: the last pictures Voyager ever took, and the most famous photo of home.
- Footage: the Pale Blue Dot (NASA/JPL, also the 2020 remaster), the family portrait mosaic.

## Ch6 OUT OF THE SUN'S BUBBLE (2012)
- Context: the Sun blows out a constant wind of particles; it makes a giant bubble around all the planets.
  Comparison: the air a moving bike pushes ahead of it.
- Idea: where the Sun's wind stops is the edge of the bubble. How do you know you have crossed it, with no
  camera? A blast from the Sun made the gas around Voyager ring like a bell, and the plasma instrument heard it:
  the gas was 40 times denser, so it was the gas between the stars.
- Payoff: 25 August 2012, the first human-made object in interstellar space. Voyager 2 followed in 2018.
- Real voice (planned): Ed Stone, the mission's project scientist for 50 years, at NASA's 2013 announcement.

## Ch7 THE SILENCE (Nov 2023 to Apr 2024): pays off the opening
- Context: what it is like to talk to Voyager: a 22-watt radio, about as bright as a fridge bulb, heard by
  dishes 70 m wide; every message takes almost a day each way.
- What broke: one chip in its 1977 computer failed; about 3% of the memory was garbage.
- The fix: a "poke" command made Voyager send back a copy of its whole memory; engineers moved the broken part of
  the program, piece by piece, into memory that still worked.
- 20 April 2024: Voyager answers in sense again. Science data followed weeks later.
- Real voice (planned): a JPL team member on that day (NASA/JPL interview). Footage: the team at JPL (NASA/JPL).

## Ch8 THE SLOW GOODBYE
- Context: the plutonium from chapter 2 makes about 4 watts less every year; by 2026 it gives less than half
  its first power.
- So the team switches instruments off, one by one, in an order agreed years ago. April 2026: the particle
  instrument goes; two are left (the magnetometer and the plasma wave instrument). The next power-saving plan is
  called, of all things, the "Big Bang" plan.
- Ed Stone, who led the science for 50 years, died in 2024.
- The milestone explained: light from the Moon takes 1.3 s, from the Sun 8 min 20 s. From Voyager 1, from 18 Nov
  2026, one full day.
- Footage: the DSN dishes, JPL control room today (NASA).

## Ending: after the last signal
- Voyager will fall silent, probably around 2030, but it won't stop. About 61,000 km/h, for ever.
- In about 40,000 years it passes within about 1.6 light-years of a star called Gliese 445.
- Callback to the opening: the machine that once sent nothing but nonsense still carries "धरती के वासियों की ओर से
  नमस्कार".
- Answer to the question: its voice ends, but the record, our message, goes on for a billion years.
- Pinned comment poll (not the narrator): "Will anyone ever find the Golden Record? 1 - yes, aliens will play it,
  2 - no one, ever, 3 - our own descendants, one day".
